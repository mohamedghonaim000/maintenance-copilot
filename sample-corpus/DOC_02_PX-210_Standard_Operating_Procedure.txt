Section 1: Equipment Overview

The PX-210 forms part of the Fluid Transfer group in the plant equipment register under asset tag PX-210-A. Its failure can interrupt the associated process train.

The equipment was selected for the duty point described in the process datasheet. Any change to flow, pressure, speed, or handled medium invalidates the assumptions in this document.

The PX-210 is installed on a rigid fabricated skid with anchoring bolts and isolation where specified. Adjacent clearances must allow safe access for inspection and removal of the guard or cover.

The unit operates at 2950 rpm under normal load conditions. Operating outside the envelope defined in Section 2 accelerates wear on bearings, seals, and drive components.

The PX-210 Centrifugal Process Pump moves process fluid from the supply tank through the distribution header to downstream consumers. It is designed for continuous duty in a ventilated industrial environment and is rated for 22 kW at its nominal operating point.

The drive train consists of a prime mover, a coupling or transmission element, and the working element of the machine. Each element contributes stored energy that must be controlled before work begins.

Section 3: Safety Prerequisites

1. Use calibrated lifting equipment for components above 25 kg and follow rigging plan for heavy lifts.
2. A second qualified person must be present for work involving pressurized line or rotating shaft.
3. Confirm the working element and shaft are completely stationary before reaching through any opening or removing any guard.
4. Never bypass, jumper, or defeat an interlock, guard switch, or protective device.
5. Confirm fire watch and hot-work permit requirements before any grinding, welding, or flame cutting nearby.
6. Barricade the work area and post a sign when guards or safety devices are removed.

Section 4: Diagnostic Procedure

1. Check mounting hardware, foundation bolts, and hold-down clamps for looseness or fretting marks.
2. Listen for bearing noise, imbalance, rubbing, or irregular knocking while the machine is running under load.
3. Inspect belts, couplings, and drive elements for glazing, cracking, misalignment, or abnormal wear patterns.
4. Review the shift log and trend data for drifting parameters that preceded the current condition.
5. Verify that lubricant levels are within the sight glass marks and that lubricant color is normal.
6. Perform a controlled stop and confirm that coast-down time is consistent with historical values.

Section 5: Maintenance Procedure

1. Examine bearings, bushings, and mounts for wear, looseness, or excessive movement; measure and record clearances.
2. Replace worn seals, gaskets, and O-rings; pressure-test or leak-check the reassembled unit before return to service.
3. Renew lubricant according to the schedule in Section 5, and verify the lubricant type against the specification table.
4. Verify alignment between driver and driven elements using the specified tolerance limits.
5. Isolate electrical power, lock out the disconnect, and tag the isolation point before any physical work.
6. Clean the work area, remove tools and consumables, and dispose of waste lubricant and parts per site procedure.
7. Function-test interlocks, sensors, and protective devices after any intervention that disturbs them.

Section 6: Common Failure Modes

Bearing-related failures account for the largest share of unplanned interventions on rotating equipment of this class. Contributing factors include contamination ingress, lubricant degradation, misalignment, and overload.

Imbalance develops from material buildup, erosion, or loss of rotating mass. Vibration spectra showing a dominant 1× running speed component point to imbalance or misalignment.

Electrical failures are frequently preceded by elevated current, hot connection points, or insulation odor. Infrared scans during normal operation are an effective screening tool.

Section 7: Troubleshooting Guide

| Symptom | Probable Causes | Recommended Action |
|---|---|---|
| Drive element slipping or glazing | Incorrect tension, contamination, pulley wear | Adjust tension; clean or replace element; inspect pulleys |
| Abnormal noise during operation | Bearing damage, rubbing contact, lubricant starvation | Stop unit; inspect bearings and clearances; re-lubricate |
| Leakage at seals or joints | Worn seals, loose fasteners, gasket damage, thermal cycling | Replace seals; re-torque to spec; renew gaskets |
| Reduced output / performance | Worn working element, internal leakage, blocked passage, speed drift | Inspect element; check internal clearances; clean passages |
| Unusual odor or smoke | Insulation overheating, lubricant breakdown, friction contact | Stop immediately; investigate before restart |
| Frequent protective trips | Relay settings, genuine overload, instrumentation fault | Review trip records; test relay; verify instrument calibration |
| Overheating | Overloading, blocked ventilation, insufficient lubricant, high ambient | Reduce load; clean openings; check lubricant; verify ambient |
| Erratic instrument readings | Sensor drift, wiring fault, moisture ingress | Calibrate sensor; inspect wiring and junctions |

Section 11: Lubrication Requirements

Lubrication intervals assume clean operating conditions and rated load. Halve the interval in dusty, hot, or humid environments.

Over-greasing is a common cause of seal failure and overheating. Apply the quantity specified in the table and purge old grease through the relief port.

Record every lubrication event with lubricant batch number in the CMMS to support warranty and failure analysis.

Verify compatibility before mixing lubricants. When changing lubricant type, flush the cavity and renew seals.

Section 12: Operating Limits and Precautions

Do not operate with guards removed, interlocks defeated, or known leaks unattended.

Frequent starts and stops stress the drive train. Allow the minimum cooldown or run-up interval specified before re-energizing.

Operating the PX-210 above its rated point reduces component life exponentially; a 10 percent overload can halve bearing life.

Monitor the trend of the parameters in Section 2 rather than absolute alarms alone; slow drift gives early warning of developing faults.

Section 13: General Workmanship Notes

Measurement guidance: Record ambient conditions together with equipment readings so that comparisons across seasons remain meaningful.

Housekeeping guidance: Dust and debris are both a fire load and a heat-insulating layer. Keep the equipment surroundings clean as a standing condition, not a periodic task.

General guidance: Always begin an intervention with a review of the equipment history. The last three work orders often reveal a pattern that isolates the current fault faster than any single measurement.

Communication guidance: Hand over open findings to the next shift in writing, including the isolation status and any parts on order.

Quality guidance: Use only parts listed in the spare parts reference. Substitute parts require engineering approval before installation.

Appendix C: Synthetic Case Studies

Case study (synthetic): Elevated current on one phase was traced to a terminal connection resistance of 4 mΩ versus the expected 0.5 mΩ. Thermal imaging during normal operation located the hot joint; re-termination resolved the imbalance.

Case study (synthetic): A unit tripped repeatedly on overload. The driven element was binding after a repair left a spacer mispositioned. Correct reassembly and a controlled restart confirmed normal current draw.
