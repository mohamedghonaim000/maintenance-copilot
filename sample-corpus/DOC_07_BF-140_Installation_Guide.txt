Section 1: Equipment Overview

Plant personnel interact with the BF-140 mainly during routine rounds, preventive maintenance, and corrective interventions. This document standardizes those activities.

An equipment file is kept at the control room containing the as-built drawings, previous maintenance records, and the modification log for the BF-140.

The drive train consists of a prime mover, a coupling or transmission element, and the working element of the machine. Each element contributes stored energy that must be controlled before work begins.

The unit operates at 3500 rpm under normal load conditions. Operating outside the envelope defined in Section 2 accelerates wear on bearings, seals, and drive components.

The BF-140 Boiler Feedwater Pump supplies treated feedwater to the boiler drum against drum operating pressure. It is designed for continuous duty in a ventilated industrial environment and is rated for 45 kW at its nominal operating point.

The BF-140 is installed on a rigid fabricated skid with anchoring bolts and isolation where specified. Adjacent clearances must allow safe access for inspection and removal of the guard or cover.

Section 3: Safety Prerequisites

1. Isolate all energy sources feeding the BF-140 and apply lockout/tagout per site procedure before accessing the equipment.
2. A second qualified person must be present for work involving high pressure water or hot piping.
3. Barricade the work area and post a sign when guards or safety devices are removed.
4. Confirm fire watch and hot-work permit requirements before any grinding, welding, or flame cutting nearby.
5. Wear safety glasses, hearing protection, gloves, and safety shoes while working in the vicinity of the equipment.
6. Verify the absence of hazardous process media — pressure, temperature, chemicals — before opening any cover or flange.

Section 4: Diagnostic Procedure

1. Check mounting hardware, foundation bolts, and hold-down clamps for looseness or fretting marks.
2. Inspect belts, couplings, and drive elements for glazing, cracking, misalignment, or abnormal wear patterns.
3. Listen for bearing noise, imbalance, rubbing, or irregular knocking while the machine is running under load.
4. Perform a controlled stop and confirm that coast-down time is consistent with historical values.
5. Measure motor current on all phases and compare with the rated value; a persistent imbalance above 5 percent requires investigation.
6. Review the shift log and trend data for drifting parameters that preceded the current condition.

Section 5: Maintenance Procedure

1. Replace worn seals, gaskets, and O-rings; pressure-test or leak-check the reassembled unit before return to service.
2. Renew lubricant according to the schedule in Section 5, and verify the lubricant type against the specification table.
3. Function-test interlocks, sensors, and protective devices after any intervention that disturbs them.
4. Isolate electrical power, lock out the disconnect, and tag the isolation point before any physical work.
5. Verify alignment between driver and driven elements using the specified tolerance limits.
6. Remove accumulated dust and debris from guards, housings, screens, and ventilation openings using low-pressure air or vacuum.
7. Examine bearings, bushings, and mounts for wear, looseness, or excessive movement; measure and record clearances.

Section 6: Common Failure Modes

Cavitation and flow recirculation damage working elements progressively. A crackling noise resembling gravel indicates cavitation risk and requires immediate review of operating conditions.

Bearing-related failures account for the largest share of unplanned interventions on rotating equipment of this class. Contributing factors include contamination ingress, lubricant degradation, misalignment, and overload.

Seal and gasket leaks rarely develop suddenly. Trend weepage during rounds; a stain that doubles between checks indicates an active leak path that will progress.

Section 7: Troubleshooting Guide

|---|---|---|

| Abnormal noise during operation | Bearing damage, rubbing contact, lubricant starvation | Stop unit; inspect bearings and clearances; re-lubricate |
| Overheating | Overloading, blocked ventilation, insufficient lubricant, high ambient | Reduce load; clean openings; check lubricant; verify ambient |

Section 11: Lubrication Requirements

Record every lubrication event with lubricant batch number in the CMMS to support warranty and failure analysis.

Over-greasing is a common cause of seal failure and overheating. Apply the quantity specified in the table and purge old grease through the relief port.

Lubrication intervals assume clean operating conditions and rated load. Halve the interval in dusty, hot, or humid environments.

Verify compatibility before mixing lubricants. When changing lubricant type, flush the cavity and renew seals.

Section 12: Operating Limits and Precautions

Monitor the trend of the parameters in Section 2 rather than absolute alarms alone; slow drift gives early warning of developing faults.

Frequent starts and stops stress the drive train. Allow the minimum cooldown or run-up interval specified before re-energizing.

Do not operate with guards removed, interlocks defeated, or known leaks unattended.

Operating the BF-140 above its rated point reduces component life exponentially; a 10 percent overload can halve bearing life.

Section 13: General Workmanship Notes

Housekeeping guidance: Dust and debris are both a fire load and a heat-insulating layer. Keep the equipment surroundings clean as a standing condition, not a periodic task.

Communication guidance: Hand over open findings to the next shift in writing, including the isolation status and any parts on order.

Measurement guidance: Record ambient conditions together with equipment readings so that comparisons across seasons remain meaningful.

General guidance: Always begin an intervention with a review of the equipment history. The last three work orders often reveal a pattern that isolates the current fault faster than any single measurement.

Quality guidance: Use only parts listed in the spare parts reference. Substitute parts require engineering approval before installation.

Appendix C: Synthetic Case Studies

Case study (synthetic): A unit tripped repeatedly on overload. The driven element was binding after a repair left a spacer mispositioned. Correct reassembly and a controlled restart confirmed normal current draw.

Case study (synthetic): A similar unit in the corpus fleet showed rising 1X vibration over six weeks. Investigation found foundation bolt looseness from thermal cycling after a process upset. Re-torquing to specification restored baseline vibration without part replacement.

Case study (synthetic): Recurring seal leaks were found to follow a lubricant change. The replacement lubricant was incompatible with the elastomer, causing swelling. Restoring the specified lubricant and renewing seals eliminated the leaks.
