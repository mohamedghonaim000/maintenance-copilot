Section 1: Equipment Overview

The drive train consists of a prime mover, a coupling or transmission element, and the working element of the machine. Each element contributes stored energy that must be controlled before work begins.

Design ambient conditions are -10 °C to +40 °C at a maximum altitude of 1000 m. Derating is required outside this envelope.

The CH-610 forms part of the Refrigeration group in the plant equipment register under asset tag CH-610-A. Its failure can interrupt the associated process train.

Operators record running hours, abnormal noise, leakage, and vibration observations in the shift log. These records form the basis for the diagnostic steps in Section 2.

Plant personnel interact with the CH-610 mainly during routine rounds, preventive maintenance, and corrective interventions. This document standardizes those activities.

The CH-610 Water-Cooled Chiller removes heat from the process cooling loop and rejects it to the condenser water circuit. It is designed for continuous duty in a ventilated industrial environment and is rated for 90 kW at its nominal operating point.

Section 3: Safety Prerequisites

1. Isolate all energy sources feeding the CH-610 and apply lockout/tagout per site procedure before accessing the equipment.
2. Wear safety glasses, hearing protection, gloves, and safety shoes while working in the vicinity of the equipment.
3. A second qualified person must be present for work involving refrigerant under pressure or electrical cabinets.
4. Never bypass, jumper, or defeat an interlock, guard switch, or protective device.
5. Use calibrated lifting equipment for components above 25 kg and follow rigging plan for heavy lifts.
6. Release stored mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, and electrical energy and verify zero energy state with a test-before-touch check.

Section 4: Diagnostic Procedure

1. Observe the equipment during a full operating cycle and compare speed, flow, pressure, and current against the expected values in Section 2.
2. Check instrumentation readings against calibrated test instruments where the installed gauge is suspected.
3. Listen for bearing noise, imbalance, rubbing, or irregular knocking while the machine is running under load.
4. Review the shift log and trend data for drifting parameters that preceded the current condition.
5. Inspect the guards, covers, and openings for dust accumulation, foreign objects, or signs of rubbing contact.
6. Perform a controlled stop and confirm that coast-down time is consistent with historical values.

Section 5: Maintenance Procedure

1. Replace worn seals, gaskets, and O-rings; pressure-test or leak-check the reassembled unit before return to service.
2. Re-torque foundation and coupling hardware to the values in the appendix after the first 50 hours of operation.
3. Reinstall guards and safety devices, confirm the area is clear, and perform a controlled restart while monitoring current, vibration, and temperature.
4. Remove accumulated dust and debris from guards, housings, screens, and ventilation openings using low-pressure air or vacuum.
5. Function-test interlocks, sensors, and protective devices after any intervention that disturbs them.
6. Renew lubricant according to the schedule in Section 5, and verify the lubricant type against the specification table.
7. Record all findings, measurements, and replaced parts in the maintenance management system before closing the work order.

Section 6: Common Failure Modes

Seal and gasket leaks rarely develop suddenly. Trend weepage during rounds; a stain that doubles between checks indicates an active leak path that will progress.

Cavitation and flow recirculation damage working elements progressively. A crackling noise resembling gravel indicates cavitation risk and requires immediate review of operating conditions.

Electrical failures are frequently preceded by elevated current, hot connection points, or insulation odor. Infrared scans during normal operation are an effective screening tool.

Section 7: Troubleshooting Guide

| Symptom | Probable Causes | Recommended Action |
|---|---|---|
| Reduced output / performance | Worn working element, internal leakage, blocked passage, speed drift | Inspect element; check internal clearances; clean passages |
| Pressure / flow instability | Cavitation risk, control instability, partial blockage | Verify suction conditions; tune controls; inspect passages |
| Corrosion or coating damage | Aggressive environment, coating breakdown, water ingress | Assess extent; repair coating; improve enclosure sealing |
| Excessive vibration | Loose mounting hardware, imbalance, misalignment, bearing wear | Re-torque mounts; check balance and alignment; inspect bearings |
| Slow or failed start | Low supply voltage, seized working element, control fault | Measure starting voltage; rotate element by hand after isolation |
| Leakage at seals or joints | Worn seals, loose fasteners, gasket damage, thermal cycling | Replace seals; re-torque to spec; renew gaskets |
| Drive element slipping or glazing | Incorrect tension, contamination, pulley wear | Adjust tension; clean or replace element; inspect pulleys |
| Premature bearing failure | Contamination, wrong lubricant, misalignment, overload | Identify root cause; correct lubrication and alignment |

Section 11: Lubrication Requirements

Record every lubrication event with lubricant batch number in the CMMS to support warranty and failure analysis.

Over-greasing is a common cause of seal failure and overheating. Apply the quantity specified in the table and purge old grease through the relief port.

Lubrication intervals assume clean operating conditions and rated load. Halve the interval in dusty, hot, or humid environments.

Verify compatibility before mixing lubricants. When changing lubricant type, flush the cavity and renew seals.

Section 12: Operating Limits and Precautions

Do not operate with guards removed, interlocks defeated, or known leaks unattended.

Operating the CH-610 above its rated point reduces component life exponentially; a 10 percent overload can halve bearing life.

Monitor the trend of the parameters in Section 2 rather than absolute alarms alone; slow drift gives early warning of developing faults.

Frequent starts and stops stress the drive train. Allow the minimum cooldown or run-up interval specified before re-energizing.

Section 13: General Workmanship Notes

Housekeeping guidance: Dust and debris are both a fire load and a heat-insulating layer. Keep the equipment surroundings clean as a standing condition, not a periodic task.

General guidance: Always begin an intervention with a review of the equipment history. The last three work orders often reveal a pattern that isolates the current fault faster than any single measurement.

Measurement guidance: Record ambient conditions together with equipment readings so that comparisons across seasons remain meaningful.

Communication guidance: Hand over open findings to the next shift in writing, including the isolation status and any parts on order.

Quality guidance: Use only parts listed in the spare parts reference. Substitute parts require engineering approval before installation.

Appendix C: Synthetic Case Studies

Case study (synthetic): A similar unit in the corpus fleet showed rising 1X vibration over six weeks. Investigation found foundation bolt looseness from thermal cycling after a process upset. Re-torquing to specification restored baseline vibration without part replacement.
